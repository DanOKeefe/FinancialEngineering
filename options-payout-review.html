1 · What the notebook does, and whether the math holds

The notebook follows the MIT OCW "Option Price and Probability Duality" lecture (Stephen Blythe). Its arc: (a) build payout functions for calls/puts, (b) combine them into call spreads and butterflies, (c) take limits to show spreads → digitals and butterflies → the risk-neutral density, (d) price real AAPL/QQQ/MSFT butterflies off a live option chain, and (e) derive that any European payoff is replicable from calls. I verified each claim independently; all of the mathematics is stated correctly.

1.1 Digital replication — verified

The portfolio of λ calls at K minus λ calls at K + 1/λ has payout ramping 0→1 over [K, K+1/λ], so its price → −∂C/∂K = Z(t,T)·P(ST > K). Cells 6 and 8 implement this correctly, and the notebook's identity P(ST < K) = 1 + (∂C/∂K)/Z is right.

note One subtlety worth a markdown cell: the [K, K+1/λ] spread under-replicates the digital (its payout is ≤ the indicator everywhere), while the [K−1/λ, K] spread over-replicates. In real markets that's the bid/ask band a dealer would quote a digital inside. For estimating the CDF from data, the centered difference (CK+ΔK − CK−ΔK)/2ΔK is second-order accurate and the better estimator (used in the demo in §3).

1.2 Butterfly → density — verified, one scaling clarification

The butterfly BK,λ = λ(CK−1/λ − 2CK + CK+1/λ) is a unit-height tent of width 2/λ centered at K (cell 11, correct). Its area is 1/λ, so its price ≈ Z·f(K)/λ — the notebook states this (minus the discount factor, see finding F3). The clarification that matters for the code: to turn a quoted butterfly price into a density estimate you must multiply by λ/Z — equivalently f(K) ≈ (CK−ΔK − 2CK + CK+ΔK) / (Z·ΔK²), the Breeden–Litzenberger (1978) formula. The notebook plots raw butterfly cost curves (cells 27, 38) whose shape is the density but whose scale is not; that's why the final cell says "need to work further on getting the implied probability."

1.3 The full loop, validated on Black–Scholes prices

A test the notebook never runs, and the single most useful cell to add: generate call prices from a known model, push them through the butterfly/spread estimators, and confirm you recover the known density. Below, Black–Scholes prices (S₀=100, r=3%, σ=25%, T=0.5) are differenced exactly as the notebook differences market quotes. The recovered density lands on the true lognormal — including with the coarse ΔK=10 grid, where you can see the smoothing bias at the peak that finite strike spacing causes (relevant to your choice of 2.5-wide vs 5-wide AAPL strikes).

1.4 Spanning: calls replicate any European payoff — derived but never implemented

Cell 40 derives g(ST) = g(0) + g′(0)ST + ∫₀∞ (ST−K)⁺ g″(K) dK and its priced form D(t,T) = Z·g(0) + g′(0)St + ∫ CK g″(K) dK. The derivation is correct (this is the Carr–Madan spanning formula). It is also the punchline for your use case — this is the recipe for turning a target payout profile into an executable option order — and the notebook ends before coding it. Here it is discretized onto a realistic $5-spaced strike grid: buy wi = g″(Ki)ΔK calls at each strike, plus g(0) bonds and g′(0) shares:

Note what the weights turn out to be: short calls where the target payoff is concave, long where convex — the generalization of the butterfly. Every "custom payout distribution" you build is, in this sense, a portfolio of butterflies with sizes chosen by g″.

Ks = np.arange(55, 165, 5.0); dK = 5.0            # tradable strike grid
g2 = (g(Ks+dK) - 2*g(Ks) + g(Ks-dK)) / dK**2      # curvature of target payoff

       + (g(1e-4)-g(0.0))/1e-4 * S                # shares
       + np.sum(w[:,None]*np.maximum(S[None,:]-Ks[:,None],0), axis=0) )
# price it straight off the chain:

2 · Findings in the notebook code

F1 · blocker Local module oc is not in the repo

cell 1 (import oc), used in cells 14, 31, 33, 37

Every data-driven cell depends on oc.get_options_chain(ticker, date), which exists only on your machine. Nobody (including future-you) can re-run the notebook. yfinance, already imported, does the same job:

t = yf.Ticker("AAPL")
expiry = t.options[0]                  # nearest listed expiry — don't hardcode dates
chain = t.option_chain(expiry)
calls = chain.calls                    # columns: strike, bid, ask, lastPrice, ...

Either commit oc.py or swap to the above. Same class of issue elsewhere in the repo: edhec_risk_kit (all 8 EDHEC notebooks) and config (Alpaca notebook — correctly left out since it holds API keys, but a config.example.py would document the expected shape).

F2 · bug Strike grid uses 10*l where it means 10*(1/l)

cell 33: Ks = np.arange(mid_strike_price-10*l, mid_strike_price+11*(1/l), 1/l)

With l = 1/5, 10*l = 2, so the grid runs from 2 dollars below the mid strike but 55 dollars above it — an asymmetric, mostly-upside grid, silently. The lower bound should be 10*(1/l). The later function get_butterfly_prices (cell 37) gets this right with plus_minus*(1/l), but the exploratory cell above it still carries the bug — worth fixing or deleting so the two don't disagree.

F3 · correctness of the estimate No discounting, no rescaling, no sanity checks on the "density"

cells 26–27, 37–38

The butterfly cost curves are plotted as-is. Three steps separate them from an implied probability distribution, and all three are missing:

  • Rescale: density ≈ λ · butterfly_price / Z. Without it the curve's units are dollars, not probability. (This is the notebook's own acknowledged TODO.)
  • Discount: Z(t,T) = e−rT appears in every formula in the markdown but never in the code. For your ~2-week AAPL horizon the bias is <0.1% — fine to drop, but say so in a comment; for the ~1-year QQQ chain in cell 38 it's a few percent.
  • Sanity checks: with bid/ask noise, individual butterflies can price negative (an implied negative probability — an apparent arbitrage that is really just a stale or wide quote). Check (prices >= 0).all(), and check the distribution integrates to ≈1: sum(density * dK) ≈ 1, equivalently sum(butterfly_prices) ≈ Z when the grid covers the support. That second identity is a beautiful no-arbitrage check that falls straight out of the math and would make a great closing cell.
lam = 1/dK
density = lam * butterfly_mid_prices / Z
assert (density >= -1e-6).all()          # flag negative implied probabilities
print("total mass:", (density * dK).sum())  # ≈ 1 if grid spans the support

F4 · estimator bias The 'real' pricing method systematically inflates the density

cells 19, 26, 37 (pricing_method='real')

Buying wings at ask and selling the body at bid means paying the full spread at all three legs of every butterfly. That adds a positive, roughly-constant premium at each strike, so the implied density is shifted up everywhere and its integral exceeds 1 — it's an execution cost model, not a probability model. You clearly sensed this, since you added the 'mid' method; I'd make mid the stated default for inference and keep 'real' only to answer "what would this bet actually cost me." (Also: the 'Last Price' variant in cell 21 mixes trades from different times of day — stale strikes make it the noisiest of the three; worth a warning comment.)

F5 · fragility Uniform-strike-spacing assumption and unguarded lookups

cells 33, 36, 37

  • l = 1/(Strike.iloc[1] - Strike.iloc[0]) infers spacing from the two deepest ITM strikes — but real chains mix spacings ($2.5 near the money, $5 further out, $1 on some underlyings). If the inferred spacing doesn't match the grid, calls[calls['Strike']==K] returns an empty frame and…
  • float(empty_array) throws an opaque TypeError — and even on a 1-element array, float(arr) is deprecated (NumPy ≥ 1.25 warns, removed later). Use .item() after asserting exactly one match, or reindex the chain by strike: calls.set_index('Strike')['Ask'].reindex(Ks) and let NaN mark the gaps.
  • A tidy fix for both: build the grid from strikes that actually exist — Ks = calls['Strike'][calls['Strike'].diff().eq(dK)]-style filtering, or intersect your desired grid with set(calls['Strike']).

F6 · reproducibility Hardcoded past expiries + "today's" spot price

cells 13–16, 31, 33, 38

The chain is pulled for fixed expiries (2020-01-31, 2021-01-15) that no longer exist, while the spot comes from history(period="1d") — i.e., today. The two are only consistent on the day the notebook was written. Pick expiries programmatically (yf.Ticker(t).options) and take the spot from the same session as the quotes. Related cosmetic issues: the cell-13 epoch-timestamp debugging (off-by-a-year discovery) and the "AAPL's closing stock price on 2019-01-22" comment (should read 2020) are leftovers worth cleaning now that the mystery is solved.

F7 · code quality option_payout nits

cell 2

  • The list comprehension [np.maximum(s-K, 0) for s in S] is O(n) Python-loop over a 1000–5000 point grid; np.maximum(S - K, 0) is the vectorized one-liner (~100×).
  • Docstring says parameter calls; the argument is call.
  • Debug print('default S') fires on every default call.
  • These are payouts, not P&L — fine and standard for this analysis, but one markdown sentence noting that premium is excluded would preempt the classic confusion when comparing to broker "profit diagrams."

3 · The step the notebook is reaching for — implied distribution, start to finish

Putting the findings together, here is the complete pipeline the notebook builds 90% of, in the notation it already uses. This is the cell I'd add after cell 38:

def implied_distribution(ticker, expiry=None, r=0.05):
    t = yf.Ticker(ticker)
    expiry = expiry or t.options[4]                 # pick a listed expiry
    calls = t.option_chain(expiry).calls
    calls = calls[(calls.bid > 0) & (calls.ask > 0)]        # drop dead quotes
    mid = calls.set_index('strike').eval('(bid + ask) / 2')

    K  = mid.index.to_numpy(); C = mid.to_numpy()
    dK = np.diff(K)
    # keep only the uniformly-spaced core of the chain (F5)
    step = pd.Series(dK).mode()[0]
    T = (pd.Timestamp(expiry) - pd.Timestamp.now()).days / 365
    Z = np.exp(-r * T)

    # Breeden–Litzenberger on mid prices (F3, F4)
    pdf = (C[:-2] - 2*C[1:-1] + C[2:]) / (Z * step**2)      # density at K[1:-1]
    cdf = 1 + (C[2:] - C[:-2]) / (2 * step * Z)             # centered spread (§1.1)

    mass = (pdf.clip(min=0) * step).sum()
    print(f"negative-density strikes: {(pdf < 0).sum()}, total mass ≈ {mass:.2f}")
    return pd.DataFrame({'pdf': pdf, 'cdf': cdf}, index=K[1:-1])

Two upgrades when you want production-quality densities rather than a demo:

  • Differentiate in vol space, not price space. Second differences amplify quote noise brutally. The standard cure: convert mids to implied vols, fit a smooth curve (spline, or an arbitrage-free parameterization like SVI), convert back to prices on a dense strike grid, then difference. Negative densities largely disappear.
  • Use OTM options on both sides. Deep-ITM calls have wide spreads; build the left tail from puts via put–call parity, the right tail from calls, stitched at the forward.
